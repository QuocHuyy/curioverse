After [Part 5](/en/notes/vqa-5-mo-hinh-da-phuong-thuc-lon/), you might think VQA is solved: strong models answer fluently, explain coherently, and even read receipts. But test them a little harder and they still fail in very systematic ways. This final part collects those weaknesses, because that is also where the research is happening.

## 1. Shortcuts never went away

In [Part 2](/en/notes/vqa-2-du-lieu-danh-gia/) we saw that early VQA models could score decently without looking at the image. VQA-CP was built to expose this: the answer distribution in the test set is deliberately different from the training set, and many models' scores dropped sharply.

The anti-shortcut methods of that era included:

- **A question-only branch** (RUBi, LMH): train a question-only model alongside, and use it to *down-weight* samples where the question alone already predicts the answer. The main model is forced to learn from the image.
- **Counterfactual data**: mask the important image region or the key word in the question, and teach the model that the answer *must* change in that case.

With large models, the problem changes shape but remains. An LLM carries very strong **background knowledge**, which sometimes overrides the image. Ask *"What color is the banana?"* about a photo of a banana painted blue, and the model may still say *"yellow"*. The MMStar benchmark was built precisely because many questions in multimodal benchmarks turned out to be answerable **without the image**.

<Callout type="tip" title="A simple test">
When evaluating a model on your data, run an extra pass with a **blank image** or an unrelated one. If the score stays high, the model is answering from background knowledge, not from its eyes.
</Callout>

## 2. Hallucination

**Hallucination** is when the model asserts things that are not in the image: describing a chair that doesn't exist, reading a number that isn't on the sign, agreeing that *"yes, there is a dog in the image"* when there is none.

### How is it measured?

- **CHAIR** (2018): the proportion of objects mentioned in a caption that are not in the image's annotations.
- **POPE** (2023): turns measurement into yes/no questions: *"Is there a chair in the image?"*. The "absent" objects are chosen in three ways: random, popular (objects that are common in general), and **adversarial** (objects that often co-occur with what is in the image, e.g. asking about a fork in a dining-table photo). The third is hardest because it hits the model's habit of "guessing from context".
- **HallusionBench** (2024): subtly edited image pairs that test whether the model trusts the image or its background knowledge.

### Why do models make things up?

1. **The LLM's background knowledge is too strong**: a dining table "usually has" a fork, so the model says there is one.
2. **Weak eyes**: the image encoder misses details, or the image is downscaled before being fed in.
3. **Flawed training data**: web captions and instruction data generated by other models sometimes contain things not in the image. The model learns that habit too.
4. **The longer the answer, the more it drifts**: towards the end of generation, the model relies more on the text it has written than on the image.

### How to reduce it

- **Cleaner data**, including negative answers (*"No, there is no … in the image"*).
- **Preference alignment**: collect (truthful answer, hallucinated answer) pairs and train with RLHF or DPO so the model prefers the truthful one (LLaVA-RLHF, RLHF-V).
- **Contrastive decoding** (Visual Contrastive Decoding): compare predictions on the original image and a noised one; tokens the model remains confident about even when the image is corrupted likely come from background knowledge, so they are penalized. No retraining needed.
- **Allow refusal** in both prompts and training data.

## 3. Counting, space, and small details

Some questions a child answers easily still trip up large models:

- **Counting**: right for 2–3 objects, increasingly wrong as numbers grow or objects overlap.
- **Spatial relations**: left/right, front/behind, above/below. Left vs right is especially confusing: from the viewpoint of the person in the image or of the viewer?
- **Subtle differences**: which way an arrow points, whether a butterfly faces left or right, what time a clock shows.

The paper *Eyes Wide Shut* (2024) pointed to an interesting cause: there are image pairs that look clearly different to people, but **the CLIP encoder gives them nearly the same vector**. If the "eyes" cannot tell them apart, the LLM behind them cannot help. Remedies include mixing features from multiple encoders (CLIP plus DINOv2, which learns purely visual features), and training encoders on data that emphasizes details.

## 4. Reading text, especially text with diacritics

Modern VLMs read English very well, but with Vietnamese they still often show:

- **Missing or wrong diacritics**: "PHỞ BÒ" becomes "PHO BO" or "PHỐ BÒ".
- **Handwriting and stylized lettering** on shop signs.
- **Complex layouts**: receipts, tables, multi-column administrative documents.

A practical trick: for documents, combine a Vietnamese-specialized OCR tool to extract the text, then give the VLM both the image and the extracted text. The model uses the image to understand the layout and the text to avoid misreading.

## 5. Multi-step reasoning

The more steps a question needs (reading a chart, computing, comparing several regions), the more likely the model fails. Three directions are being pursued:

**Chain-of-thought on images.** Ask the model to write out its steps before concluding. Simple, and often helpful for math and charts, but long reasoning chains also open more room for hallucination.

**Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards.** Following the success of text reasoning models, since 2025 many groups have applied reinforcement learning (often GRPO) to VLMs: the model generates its own solutions, is rewarded when the final answer is correct (automatically checkable for math, counting or multiple choice), and gradually learns to reason longer and more carefully. Works such as Vision-R1, OpenVLThinker and Visionary-R1 report clear gains on MathVista and other reasoning benchmarks.

**"Thinking with images".** Instead of reasoning only in text, the model may **operate on the image** midway: zoom into a region, crop it, mark coordinates, then look again. This is what people do when reading small print. Some commercial models and works like GRIT go in this direction.

### An old branch returns: visual programming

Remember module networks and NS-VQA from [Part 3](/en/notes/vqa-3-kien-truc-co-dien/)? The idea of "translating the question into a program" came back with **VisProg** and **ViperGPT** (2023): an LLM writes Python that calls vision tools (object detection, counting, distance measurement, OCR), then runs that code.

```python
# Question: "Are there more cups than plates?"
cups = find(image, "cup")
plates = find(image, "plate")
answer = "yes" if len(cups) > len(plates) else "no"
```

Pros: every step is explainable, counting and comparison are more accurate. Cons: it depends on tool quality, and a later analysis showed that part of the reported advantage came from choosing a tool set suited to each benchmark. Today this direction mostly appears as **agents** that call tools.

## 6. When the answer lies outside the image

For knowledge questions (OK-VQA, A-OKVQA, or *"Which province is this pagoda in?"*), there are two options: trust what the LLM has learned, or **go and look it up**. The second is **multimodal RAG**: use the image and question to retrieve documents or similar images from a collection, then answer based on what was found. Many lessons from text RAG for Vietnamese (Unicode normalization, chunking, evaluation) carry over here.

## 7. Evaluation is a challenge too

- **Data leakage**: models trained on web data may have already "seen" benchmark questions. A high score is not necessarily real ability.
- **Saturation**: any popular benchmark quickly gets optimized to the point where it no longer separates models.
- **LLM judges**: convenient but biased (favoring longer answers, or answers in their own style). Validate with human judgments on a small sample.
- **The gap to real applications**: user photos (like VizWiz) are blurry, tilted and dark, far from benchmark images.

## 8. Vietnamese and other low-resource languages

The difficulties I see most clearly:

- **Little data, small datasets** compared to English; some of it is translated, carrying translation errors and unnatural phrasing.
- **Cultural context**: Vietnamese food, clothing, signs and festivals are rare in the training data of large models.
- **Token cost**: many LLM tokenizers handle Vietnamese less efficiently than English, so the same sentence costs more tokens.
- **Evaluation**: as discussed in Part 2, word segmentation and diacritic normalization need to be agreed on.

This is also an opportunity. A small but clean dataset in a specific domain (receipts, traffic signs, textbooks, Vietnamese medical images), together with LoRA fine-tuning of an open VLM, can already produce useful results and a valuable contribution.

## 9. Beyond: video, 3D and robots

VQA on still images is only the first step. Natural extensions:

- **Video QA**: *"What does this person do after opening the fridge?"* requires temporal understanding, and the token count explodes with the number of frames.
- **Embodied QA**: an agent must move through an environment to find the answer.
- **3D spatial understanding**: distances, real sizes, room layouts.
- **Vision–language–action models** for robots, where the "answer" is an action.

## A self-study roadmap

If you want to go deep into VQA, here is the order I suggest:

| Stage | Content | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Foundations | CNNs, Transformers, attention, PyTorch | Implement attention from scratch; read *Attention Is All You Need* and ViT |
| 2. Classic VQA | VQA v2, BUTD, Tips and Tricks | Run the Part 3 baseline; measure the question-only baseline |
| 3. Pretraining | CLIP, ViLT, BLIP, BLIP-2 | Try zero-shot with CLIP; compare ViLT and BLIP as in Part 4 |
| 4. VLMs | LLaVA, LLaVA-1.5, one technical report (Qwen-VL or InternVL) | Run an open VLM; LoRA fine-tune on a small dataset, e.g. part of ViTextVQA |
| 5. Evaluation and reliability | POPE, MMStar, hallucination surveys | Build a small test set with trap questions; use VLMEvalKit or lmms-eval |
| 6. Pick a direction | Reasoning, documents, medicine, Vietnamese, video… | Read the 5–10 most recent papers in that direction and reproduce one result |

## Closing the series

Looking back, VQA has come full circle in an interesting way. It started as a classification problem with carefully hand-designed blocks, and gradually dissolved into general-purpose models where "asking an image a question" is just one of many things they do. But the original questions remain: does the model **really look**, or does it guess by habit? Does it know **when it doesn't know**? And can it understand **our** images, with Vietnamese diacritics, food, streets and ways of speaking?

Those are questions worth staying curious about.
