By the end of [Part 4](/en/notes/vqa-4-transformer-tien-huan-luyen/) we had Flamingo and BLIP-2: take a large language model (LLM) and teach it to read images. In 2023, LLaVA showed the recipe could be **simpler still**, and that kicked off a whole generation of models known as **VLMs** (Vision-Language Models) or **MLLMs** (Multimodal LLMs).

In this generation, VQA is no longer a separate "task" with its own architecture. It becomes **one capability** of a multimodal assistant: you hand it an image, ask in natural language, and the model answers, explains, or declines if unsure.

## The LLaVA recipe

**LLaVA** (Large Language and Vision Assistant, 2023) has three components:

```text
image ─► [Image encoder: CLIP ViT] ─► patch vectors ─► [Projection] ─► image tokens ─┐
                                                                                    ├─► [LLM] ─► answer
                                                  question ─► text tokens ──────────┘
```

1. **Image encoder**: CLIP ViT-L/14, producing one vector per patch (576 patches, 24×24, for a 336×336 image).
2. **Projection**: originally a single linear matrix; LLaVA-1.5 switched to a two-layer MLP. Its job: map patch vectors to the LLM's embedding size.
3. **LLM**: Vicuna (a fine-tuned LLaMA).

No Q-Former, no cross-attention, no resampler. Image tokens are **placed directly before** the text tokens, and the LLM uses ordinary self-attention to read both. From the LLM's point of view, the image is just a few hundred strange "words" at the start of the prompt.

<Callout type="insight" title="Why is something this simple enough?">
Two reasons. First, the CLIP encoder already provides semantic features close to the space of language, so a small projection is enough to "translate". Second, LLMs are very good at exploiting context, even unusual context. LLaVA's big lesson is that **data matters more than architecture**: the paper's main contribution is how it created its training data.
</Callout>

### Two-stage training

| Stage | Data | What is trained | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Feature alignment | About 558 thousand image–caption pairs | Projection only | Teach the projection to "translate" image vectors into the LLM's language |
| 2. Visual instruction tuning | About 158 thousand conversations (LLaVA), 665 thousand mixed samples (LLaVA-1.5) | Projection + LLM | Teach the model to follow instructions, converse and reason about images |

### How was the instruction data created?

This is the clever part. In 2023 there were few models that could see images well, so the authors used **text-only GPT-4**: they gave it the captions and object bounding boxes of a COCO image and asked it to write three kinds of data:

- **Conversations**: multi-turn Q&A about the image.
- **Detailed descriptions**: a paragraph describing the image thoroughly.
- **Complex reasoning**: questions that require multi-step inference.

LLaVA-1.5 added data from academic VQA datasets (VQA v2, GQA, OK-VQA, TextVQA…) in instruction form, with the prompt *"Answer the question using a single word or phrase."* That small prompt resolves the conflict between long conversational answers and short benchmark answers, and gave a clear boost in VQA scores.

## High-resolution images

336×336 is far too small for documents, charts or text on signs. But ViT encoders are trained at a fixed resolution. There are three common approaches:

| Approach | Idea | Representatives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tiling** | Cut a large image into standard-sized tiles, encode each, and add a downscaled thumbnail of the whole image to keep the layout | LLaVA-NeXT (AnyRes), InternVL (dynamic tiling) |
| **Native resolution** | Train the ViT to take images of any size, with 2D position encoding; the token count varies with image size | Qwen2-VL and later |
| **Token compression** | Merge neighboring patches (e.g. 2×2 into 1) to reduce how many tokens the LLM must read | Pixel shuffle, pooling, resamplers |

The trade-off is always **token count**: the sharper the image, the more the LLM must read, the slower and more memory-hungry it gets. A legible A4 document can cost a few thousand tokens.

## Two architectural schools

| | Image tokens in the sequence (LLaVA-style) | Cross-attention (Flamingo-style) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How the LLM reads the image | Self-attention over a shared sequence | Extra inserted cross-attention layers |
| Changes to the LLM | Architecture unchanged | New layers added |
| Cost for large images | Long sequences, expensive | Text sequence doesn't grow |
| Prevalence | Dominant among open models today | Some models (Flamingo, Llama 3.2 Vision) |

## Training a modern VLM

Models after LLaVA usually have a longer pipeline:

1. **Alignment**: train only the bridge, like LLaVA stage 1.
2. **Large-scale multimodal pretraining**: hundreds of millions to billions of samples including captions, interleaved web image–text documents, OCR, charts, tables, object grounding. The image encoder is often unfrozen.
3. **Instruction tuning**: conversations, VQA, documents, math, code, many languages.
4. **Alignment with user preferences**: RLHF, DPO, and more recently reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards to teach multi-step reasoning (Part 6).

## A map of model families

The field moves very fast, so instead of listing versions I note the **families** worth knowing. Before choosing, check the latest model card and the license terms.

| Model family | Developer | Notable traits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **LLaVA** (1.5, NeXT, OneVision) | Academic community | The original recipe, open code and data, the base for a lot of research |
| **Qwen-VL** (Qwen2.5-VL, Qwen3-VL…) | Alibaba | Native resolution, strong on documents, OCR, grounding; many sizes; fairly good multilingual support |
| **InternVL** (2.5, 3, 3.5…) | Shanghai AI Lab | Dynamic tiling, high benchmark scores among open models |
| **Gemma 3**, **PaliGemma** | Google | SigLIP encoder; PaliGemma is designed to be fine-tuned for specific tasks |
| **Molmo** | Allen AI | Open training data too, can "point" at locations in the image |
| **SmolVLM** | Hugging Face | Very small, runs on modest hardware |
| **Llama 3.2 Vision** | Meta | Cross-attention style |
| Commercial models | OpenAI, Google, Anthropic… | Strongest overall, used through APIs, architectures not disclosed in detail |

<Sidenote>For Vietnamese, test on your own data rather than trusting leaderboards. A model at the top of English benchmarks may still misread diacritics, or answer in a mix of English or Chinese.</Sidenote>

## Benchmarks for this generation

VQA v2 is nearly saturated, so current reports use a "basket" of benchmarks:

| Group | Examples | What it measures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General | MMBench, MMStar, SEED-Bench | Many skills, multiple choice |
| Expert | MMMU, MMMU-Pro | College-level knowledge with images |
| Documents, text | DocVQA, ChartQA, InfoVQA, OCRBench | Reading and understanding text in images |
| Math | MathVista, MathVerse | Math reasoning with figures |
| Hallucination | POPE, HallusionBench | Does it make up things that aren't there? |
| Video | Video-MME, MVBench | Temporal understanding |

Tools like **VLMEvalKit** and **lmms-eval** run many benchmarks through one pipeline, and the OpenCompass leaderboard aggregates results for a large number of models.

## Hands-on: VQA with an open VLM

The code below uses `transformers` (version 5) with a 3-billion-parameter Qwen2.5-VL model, which runs on a GPU with roughly 8–12 GB. You can swap `model_id` for another model of the same kind (`AutoModelForImageTextToText`). I checked the function names against the library, but could not run it end to end because the environment I wrote this in could not download model weights.

```python
import torch
from transformers import AutoProcessor, AutoModelForImageTextToText

model_id = "Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct"
processor = AutoProcessor.from_pretrained(model_id)
model = AutoModelForImageTextToText.from_pretrained(
    model_id, dtype=torch.bfloat16, device_map="auto"
)


def ask(image_path: str, question: str, max_new_tokens: int = 128) -> str:
    messages = [
        {
            "role": "user",
            "content": [
                {"type": "image", "path": image_path},
                {"type": "text", "text": question},
            ],
        }
    ]
    inputs = processor.apply_chat_template(
        messages,
        add_generation_prompt=True,
        tokenize=True,
        return_dict=True,
        return_tensors="pt",
    ).to(model.device)
    out = model.generate(**inputs, max_new_tokens=max_new_tokens, do_sample=False)
    # Drop the prompt, keep only the newly generated tokens
    new_tokens = out[0, inputs["input_ids"].shape[1]:]
    return processor.decode(new_tokens, skip_special_tokens=True)


print(ask("pho.jpg", "How many bowls of phở are in the image? Answer with a number only."))
print(ask("pho.jpg", "What price is on the sign? If you can't read it, say 'unclear'."))
```

### Prompting tips

- **Force the format** when you need a short answer: *"Answer with one word"*, *"Answer yes or no"*. Long answers are hard to score and tend to ramble.
- **Allow "I don't know"**: *"If the image doesn't contain enough information, say unclear."* Without this, models tend to guess.
- **Describe first, answer second** for hard questions: ask the model to list what is relevant in the image before concluding. This is a form of chain-of-thought.
- **Crop and zoom** into the region you need to read if the text is small. The model doesn't always see sharply enough, especially when the image is downscaled to limit the token count.
- **Disable sampling** (`do_sample=False`) when evaluating, so results are reproducible.

## Fine-tuning for your task

When an off-the-shelf model isn't good enough (e.g. specialist images, Vietnamese documents with a particular layout), fine-tuning is the next step:

1. **Prepare data** in the conversation format above: each sample is an image, a question and an answer. A few thousand high-quality samples usually beat tens of thousands of noisy ones.
2. **Use LoRA or QLoRA**: train only a small fraction of the parameters, enough to fit on one GPU. The TRL (`SFTTrainer`) and PEFT libraries have ready-made VLM examples.
3. **Usually keep the image encoder frozen**, fine-tuning only the LLM and the projection, unless your images are very different from ordinary photos.
4. **Keep a fixed test set** and measure before and after. Also check whether the model "forgets" its general abilities.

## Evaluating on your own data

A compact workflow I like:

1. Collect 100–300 (image, question, answer) triples representative of what you actually need.
2. Split them by question type: counting, reading, attributes, reasoning.
3. Score short answers by matching after normalization (lowercase, Unicode NFC, strip punctuation); use an LLM judge for long answers, and hand-check a sample to validate the judge.
4. Add a few **trap** questions about things that are not in the image (*"What color is the cat?"* when there is no cat) to measure hallucination.

## Summary

- LLaVA: CLIP encoder + projection + LLM, with image tokens placed directly in the sequence. Simple, and it proved instruction data is the key.
- Sharp images are handled by tiling, native resolution, or token compression. The trade-off is token count.
- There are many strong open model families (Qwen-VL, InternVL, Gemma, LLaVA…); always test on your own data, especially for Vietnamese.
- Forcing the format, allowing "I don't know", and evaluating systematically are three key habits when using VLMs for VQA.
