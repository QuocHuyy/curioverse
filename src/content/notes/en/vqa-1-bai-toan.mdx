Picture a familiar scene: a Hanoi street corner in the morning, a sidewalk phở stall, a few motorbikes parked by the curb, and a sign that reads *"Phở bò 40k"*.

You could ask this picture many things:

- *How many motorbikes are there?* (counting)
- *What color is the nearest bike?* (attributes)
- *How much is a bowl of phở?* (reading text in the image)
- *What is the person on the left doing?* (action and position)
- *Is this breakfast?* (inference from context)
- *Where might this dish come from?* (knowledge beyond the image)

People answer these almost instantly. Teaching a machine to do the same is the task called **Visual Question Answering**, or **VQA**: given an image and a question in natural language, produce the correct answer.

## A compact definition

Let $I$ be the image, $Q$ the question, $A$ the answer. A VQA system is a function

$$
f(I, Q) \to A
$$

Simple enough, but there are two ways to read $A$, and they shaped the whole history of the field:

| View | What $A$ is | Example | When it dominated |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Classification** | A label from a fixed answer set, usually the ~3,000 most frequent answers | `"2"`, `"red"`, `"yes"` | 2015–2021 |
| **Generation** | Any sequence of words the model writes itself | `"Two, one red and one black"` | Roughly 2021 onward |

Under the **classification** view, we learn a probability distribution over the answer set:

$$
\hat{a} = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \; p(a \mid I, Q)
$$

where $\mathcal{A}$ is the fixed answer set. This is easy to train and easy to score, but it cannot answer anything whose answer lies outside $\mathcal{A}$.

Under the **generation** view, the answer is produced token by token, like a language model:

$$
p(A \mid I, Q) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(a_t \mid a_{<t}, I, Q)
$$

This is far more flexible (long answers, explanations, reading arbitrary text), at the cost of harder scoring. Part 2 covers scoring in detail.

<Callout type="insight" title="Another way to see it">
VQA is often described as a kind of "visual Turing test". A system that answers *any* question about an image well almost has to understand that image, because a question can touch any aspect of it: objects, attributes, positions, text, relations, even things that are not directly visible.
</Callout>

## How is VQA different from other vision tasks?

To see where VQA gets hard, put it next to its siblings:

| Task | Input | Output | Who decides what to look at? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Image classification | Image | One label | Fixed at design time |
| Object detection | Image | Boxes + labels | Fixed (a list of classes) |
| Captioning | Image | One descriptive sentence | The model picks what stands out |
| **VQA** | Image + question | Answer | **The question**, and it changes every time |

The key is the last column. A classifier always does *one* thing. A captioning model is free to talk about whatever is most obvious. A VQA model must let the question steer its looking: ask about the price of phở and it has to find the sign and read it; ask about motorbikes and it has to find and count them. VQA is really **many vision tasks bundled together**, and the model doesn't know in advance which one it will face.

## Why VQA is hard

### 1. It has to bridge two worlds

An image is a continuous grid of pixels. A question is a discrete sequence of words. The model has to bring two very different things into a shared space and let them "talk". This is the central question of **multimodal learning**, and each generation of VQA answers it differently: from concatenating two vectors (Part 3), to cross-attention between image and text (Part 4), to turning the image into "tokens" that a large language model reads (Part 5).

### 2. It needs many skills at once

A question like *"What is the man to the left of the red car holding?"* requires recognizing the man, recognizing the car, identifying "red", understanding "to the left of", then recognizing the held object. Five steps; get one wrong and the whole answer is wrong.

### 3. It is easy to cheat

This is the most famous trap. In the early datasets, for *"Is there …?"* questions, answering *"yes"* was right most of the time. For *"How many…?"*, guessing *"2"* scored surprisingly well. A model that **never looks at the image**, reading only the question, still reaches a striking accuracy. Many VQA models look brilliant on paper while actually learning the statistics of language rather than learning to see.<Sidenote>This is called a *language prior* or *language bias*. Parts 2 and 6 come back to it, because it never fully went away, not even with today's large models.</Sidenote>

### 4. There is more than one right answer

Ask *"What is this person doing?"*: one person says *"riding a bike"*, another *"cycling"*, a third *"going for a ride"*. Who is right? Scoring a free-form answer is a problem of its own.

### 5. Sometimes the answer is not in the image

*"Which country is this kind of vehicle common in?"* The image shows only the vehicle. The rest is **world knowledge**, which the model must already have or must go and find.

## Common question types

A rough taxonomy, good enough for reading papers:

| Type | Example | Main skill |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yes/No | *Is there a cat?* | Recognition, verification |
| Counting | *How many people?* | Detection + counting (harder than it looks) |
| Attributes | *What color is the shirt?* | Attribute recognition |
| Position, relations | *What is on the table?* | Spatial understanding |
| Reading text | *What does the sign say?* | Scene-text OCR |
| Inference | *Is it about to rain?* | Combining several cues |
| External knowledge | *When was this building built?* | World knowledge |
| Documents, charts | *What was Q3 revenue?* | Reading layouts, tables, plots |

## Who uses VQA?

- **Assisting blind and low-vision people.** Perhaps the most meaningful application. The VizWiz dataset was built from *real* questions that blind people asked by taking a photo on their phone: *"What flavor is this box?"*, *"When does this medicine expire?"*. The photos are often blurry, tilted or dark, and some questions cannot be answered from the photo at all. Very different from clean lab images.
- **Document understanding.** Q&A over invoices, contracts, forms, and report charts (DocVQA, ChartQA).
- **Medicine.** Q&A over X-rays and CT scans (VQA-RAD, PathVQA), usually as an assistant to clinicians, not a replacement.
- **E-commerce, education.** *"Does this shirt have pockets?"*, solving a geometry problem from a photo.
- **Robots and autonomous vehicles.** Asking about the surroundings as one step in planning.
- **Multimodal AI assistants.** When you photograph something and ask a chatbot about it, that is VQA in its most general form.

| Period | Main idea | Representatives | Read in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2014–2015 | The task is born, first datasets | DAQUAR, COCO-QA, VQA v1 | Part 2 |
| 2015–2018 | CNN for images + LSTM for questions, then fusion and attention | LSTM Q + I, SAN, MCB, Bottom-Up Top-Down | Part 3 |

## Summary

- VQA: given an image $I$ and a question $Q$, find the answer $A$.
- Two views: **classification** over a fixed answer set, or free-form **generation**. The field moved from the first to the second.
- The difficulty: the question decides what to look at, many skills are needed at once, and models readily cheat by learning language statistics.
